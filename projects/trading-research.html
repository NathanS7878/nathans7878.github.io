Systematic Trading Research & Execution System

A quantitative research pipeline that tests strategies honestly, and the semi-automated execution system built on the one that survived.

Maximum drawdown −30% versus −55% for buy-and-hold, at roughly matched CAGR, over 33 years spanning four major crashes.

Most of this project is a negative result, and that's the point. Four classic intraday strategies were tested honestly and none of them worked. Reporting that is more useful than another backtest curve.

The problem

Backtests lie. Lookahead bias, survivorship bias, untested parameters, and ignored transaction costs make almost any strategy look profitable on paper. The goal here was to build a pipeline where those failure modes are structurally impossible, then see what survives.

What survived is smaller than what most backtests claim, and the gap between those two things is the actual finding.

How it works

  • Pure Python, standard library only — no pandas or numpy in the core. Indicators, the backtest loop, and the risk metrics are hand-implemented so every calculation is inspectable.
  • No lookahead, enforced by design. A signal is computed on a bar's close and executes at the next bar's open. No bar can inform a trade that happens inside it.
  • Costs on every fill. One basis point of slippage per intraday fill, five basis points per unit of turnover at each swing rebalance. Retail commissions are $0 on the brokers used, so the modeled friction is slippage and spread.
  • Chronological 60/40 train/test split, with the full parameter surface reported — every SMA window from 100–250 days, every top-K from 2–4 — rather than the single best-performing setting. That way a reader can see whether a result is a ridge or a spike.
  • A survivorship-bias-free universe. The nine original Select Sector SPDR ETFs form a complete, non-overlapping partition of the S&P 500 with common history back to 1998. XLRE (2015) and XLC (2018) were excluded on purpose so the basket's history still spans every major crash of the last 25 years.

What I measured

Full sample 1998–2026, daily, net of modeled costs. Strategy selection was made on the earlier 60% of the sample and validated on the held-out 40%; the table below is the full period, reported for comparability.

Two things worth calling out

A +48% result was thrown away. A TSLA breakout strategy showed +48% in the test window. It was rejected as multiple-testing noise, because it lost money in-sample and therefore could never have been selected in advance. It is not in the results.

A broker API was serving fabricated prices. One data source returned interpolated synthetic 5-minute bars beyond roughly 4.5 months of real history. These were detected and filtered so no backtest ever ran on prices that didn't happen.

Deployment

The surviving strategy runs as a scheduled, human-in-the-loop system: a monthly signal generator refreshes data and emits the target allocation, and a rebalance calculator reads live brokerage positions and computes exact fractional-share orders behind a review → type-to-confirm gate.

Execution safety is layered: a DRY_RUN mode, a tradable-ticker allowlist, a per-order dollar cap, a sub-$1 no-trade band, sells-before-buys sequencing, and per-order error handling that never leaves the account in an unknown state. Building it surfaced a real bug — the brokerage library defaulted to the wrong account — which is now handled by explicit account routing. Credentials live in a gitignored env file.

Limitations

  • It did not beat the market. Dual momentum underperformed buy-and-hold on CAGR by about 1.1 points a year. It is crash insurance, and the foregone return is the premium.
  • The Sharpe difference is not statistically significant. 0.69 against 0.65 sounds like a win, but the standard error on a Sharpe estimate over 27 years is roughly 0.21. A 0.04 gap is noise. The drawdown reduction — −55% to −30% — is the result I would actually defend.
  • The edge is concentrated in crash regimes. In the calmer, most recent out-of-sample window, plain buy-and-hold wins on Sharpe. The strategy earns its keep in 2000, 2008, and 2020 and drags the rest of the time.
  • Published support doesn't fully escape selection bias. Dual momentum is Antonacci's published strategy, tested here on the asset class it was published on. The academic literature is itself survivorship-biased — strategies that failed didn't become papers. So the out-of-sample split tests my parameter choices more than it tests the underlying hypothesis.
  • No market-impact modeling. The flat slippage haircut is defensible for instruments as liquid as SPY and the sector SPDRs. It would not be for small caps.
  • Setting the risk-free rate to zero across 1998–2026 penalizes a strategy that sits in cash during downturns, so the reported Sharpe is, if anything, conservative for dual momentum.

Built for research and personal use. Backtested results are historical and are not a prediction of future performance. The live component is a semi-automated, human-approved system, not an autonomous trading bot. Nothing here is investment advice.
